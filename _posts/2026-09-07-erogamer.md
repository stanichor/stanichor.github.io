Answer: Probably not. But there's a twist.

Now that we've got the boring stuff out of the way, let's get to the interesting parts. Why do people think Yudkowsky wrote *The Erogamer*? I don't know. I've never read *The Erogamer*[^inexperience]. As far as I can tell, it's some sort of story that takes the form of an erotic litRPG and features (LessWrong) rationalist topics. Yudkowsky is known for writing... well, if you've read *Planecrash*, you can see why that description would make people suspect Yudkowsky of being the author.

Why do *I* think Yudkowsky probably didn't write *The Erogamer*? [Stylometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stylometry). You've probably heard of stylometry in the context of [LLMs being able to determine the authors of texts](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Jkb4CBB7rf4XYP5eb/claude-knows-who-you-are). That's what it is: determining who authored a piece of text[^attribution]. However, I won't be using anything as fancy as an LLM with billions (trillions?) of parameters. I'm going to use a very simple method that just makes use of a few hundred: Burrows's Delta.

Now, I've mentioned Burrows's Delta before, in my post on [galaxy-brained explanations](/galaxy-brain/). Burrows's Delta is a method that takes the $n$ most frequent words (MFWs), converts their raw frequencies in each text into standardized scores, then computes a distance between documents, which we can use to determine who wrote a text if, for example, we have sample texts whose authorship we already know (for more details, [see the Appendix](#stylometry-process)). To add a bit of robustness and test sensitivity to this choice, I vary $n$, using comparison vectors made from the 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 most frequent words.

I've gathered various works of fiction written by ratfic authors and *Worm* fanfic authors (because Groon the Walker previously wrote a *Worm* fic). Here are the documents that are closest to *The Erogamer*, with bold entries indicating reciprocity (*The Erogamer* is among that work's five closest as well):

So, it looks like our top candidate is not Yudkowsky but DaystarEld. But, being a rationalist, I can’t help wondering exactly how confident I should be that DaystarEld wrote *The Erogamer*. This is where Bayes comes into play. For each candidate author $a$, I’ve calculated $P(a\text{ is the true author} \mid C_a=c)$, where $C_a$ denotes the minimum rank attained by any work by $a$, and $c\in\lbrace1, 2\text{–}5, >5\rbrace$.

As you can see, *if* the author of *The Erogamer* is one of the 14 authors listed above, then the model's median probability across the five MFW settings is 94.7% for DaystarEld and 1.3% for Yudkowsky. It seems open and shut: Yudkowsky did not write *The Erogamer*. If we're going to point the finger at anyone, we should point it at DaystarEld.

However, there's something interesting I noticed. If we look at the other work attributed to Groon the Walker, [*Taylor Hebert, Harem Protagonist*](#taylor-hebert-harem-protagonist-check), we see that *The Erogamer* doesn't appear among its top five closest works, no matter the MFW setting. Additionally, its closest work across all settings is *Harry Potter and the Methods of Rationality*, written by none other than Eliezer Yudkowsky. Using *The Erogamer* as Groon the Walker's comparison text and a uniform prior over 15 candidate authors, the model's median probability across the five MFW settings is 95.0% for Yudkowsky and 0.17% for ~~Groon the Walker~~ the same author as *The Erogamer*.

In summary, my conclusion after this analysis is that Yudkowsky *did not* write *The Erogamer*. But he's probably the author behind the other work attributed to Groon the Walker: *Taylor Hebert, Harem Protagonist*.

*Note: The above probabilities are upper bounds because they assume the author of the work I'm investigating is in our candidate set. So, if you think the true author is someone I haven't considered, you can contact me and give me the author's name, as well as the works you want me to analyze.*

## Appendix

### Stylometry Process

I first extracted word tokens, defined as maximal sequences of Unicode letters, converted to lowercase. Internal apostrophes were retained so that contractions could remain whole tokens, and the various Unicode apostrophe characters were normalized to the ASCII apostrophe (`'`). Hyphens and other punctuation terminate tokens. Numeric strings and lexical runs containing numbers were discarded. URLs, markup, embeds, site chrome, reader comments, author notes, polls, and similar non-fiction material were removed where identifiable.

A predefined list of personal pronouns (e.g., `i'm`, `you've`, and `they'll`) was excluded. This was intended to reduce the extent to which the analysis distinguishes first-person from third-person narration rather than authorship.

The primary analysis was restricted to works containing more than 5,000 cleaned tokens. For token $t$ in work $w$, its relative frequency was defined as

$$
f_{w,t}=
\frac{\operatorname{count}(t\text{ in }w)}
{\operatorname{total tokens in }w}.
$$

To rank candidate features, I first calculated each author’s mean relative frequency:

$$
f_{a,t}=
\frac{1}{n_a}\sum_{w\in a}f_{w,t},
$$

where $n_a$ is the number of eligible works by author $a$. I then averaged these author-level frequencies:

$$
\bar f_t=
\frac{1}{A}\sum_a f_{a,t}.
$$

Tokens were ranked by $\bar f_t$, so every author received equal weight. Within an author, every work received equal weight regardless of length.

Before selecting features, tokens were discarded if either their work dominance or author dominance exceeded 50%. Work dominance was defined as

$$
D_t^{(\mathrm{work})}=
\frac{\max_w f_{w,t}}
{\sum_w f_{w,t}},
$$

and author dominance as

$$
D_t^{(\mathrm{author})}=
\frac{\max_a f_{a,t}}
{\sum_a f_{a,t}}.
$$

Separate analyses used the highest-ranked 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 surviving tokens. For a particular feature set, each work initially had a relative-frequency vector

$$
\mathbf{x}_w=
[f_{w,t_1},f_{w,t_2},\ldots,f_{w,t_m}].
$$

For each token, relative frequencies were z-scored across eligible works using the population standard deviation. The resulting values were ternarized using the standard-normal tertile cutoff \(c=\Phi^{-1}(2/3)\approx0.4307\):

$$
q_{w,t}=
\begin{cases}
-1,&z_{w,t}<-c,\\
0,&-c\le z_{w,t}\le c,\\
+1,&z_{w,t}>c.
\end{cases}
$$

Similarity was then measured using cosine distance between the resulting ternary vectors:

$$
d(\mathbf q_i,\mathbf q_j)=
1-\frac{\mathbf q_i\cdot\mathbf q_j}
{\lVert\mathbf q_i\rVert\lVert\mathbf q_j\rVert}.
$$

### Bayesian Calibration Model

We analyze each MFW setting separately. Each work was paired with each of the 14 candidate authors. For each candidate author $a$, I record:

$$
C_a \in \{1, 2\text{–}5, >5\}
$$

where $C_a$ is the *minimum* rank occupied by any work attributed to author $a$. I also record whether author $a$ is the work's actual author: $z=\text{correct}$ when it is, and $z=\text{incorrect}$ otherwise.

For each source author $s$ (the known author of the work being tested) and status $z \in \lbrace \text{correct}, \text{incorrect} \rbrace$, the model is:

$$
\boldsymbol{\mu}_z \sim \operatorname{Dirichlet}(1,1,1)
$$

$$
\kappa_z \sim \operatorname{LogNormal}(\log 3, 1)
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s,z}
\sim
\operatorname{Dirichlet}(\kappa_z \boldsymbol{\mu}_z)
$$

$$
\mathbf{n}_{s,z}
\sim
\operatorname{Multinomial}(N_{s,z}, \boldsymbol{\theta}_{s,z})
$$

where:
- $\boldsymbol{\mu}_z$ is the population-level distribution over the three rank categories. 
- $\kappa_z$ controls how similar the source authors are to one another.
- $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s,z}$ is the source author $s$'s rank-category distribution.
- $\mathbf{n}_{s,z}$ contains, for source author $s$, the observed counts in rank 1, ranks 2-5, and ranks >5.

In the actual PyMC implementation, $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ is analytically marginalized, producing a Dirichlet-multinomial likelihood. After sampling the hyperparameters, we can generate conditional posterior draws:

$$
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s,z} \mid \text{data} \sim \operatorname{Dirichlet}\left(\mathbf{n}_{s,z} + \kappa_{z}\boldsymbol{\mu}_z\right)
$$

I then average the rank-category distributions equally over the 14 authors:

$$
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{z} = \frac{1}{14}\sum_s \boldsymbol{\theta}_{s,z}
$$

Right now, we're able to estimate $P(C = c \mid \text{correct})$ and $P(C = c \mid \text{incorrect})$, but we want to be able to estimate $P(\text{correct} \mid C = c)$. For this, we need the power of Bayes. We'll apply the principle of indifference and assume each author is equally likely, that is, $P(\text{correct}) = \frac{1}{14}$ and $P(\text{incorrect}) = \frac{13}{14}$. Bayes' rule tells us:

$$
P(\text{correct} | C = c) = \frac{P(C = c \mid \text{correct})}{P(C = c \mid \text{correct}) + 13P(C = c \mid \text{incorrect})}
$$
